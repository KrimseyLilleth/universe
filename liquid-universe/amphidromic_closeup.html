Amphidromic point: close-up and wide view

view

A round sea about 400 km across, with the M2 amphidromic point at its centre (the red post).

Near the point, the sea surface is a tilted flat sheet. The tilt direction turns once every 12.42 h, like a plate wobbling on a pivot. The centre is the pivot, so its level never changes.

A North Sea-sized channel, 1400 km long and 450 km wide, 60 m deep, at 55°N.

The tide comes in as a Kelvin wave (Earth's rotation pushes it against the coast on its right). It reflects off the far end and comes back hugging the other coast. Where the two waves cancel, you get a row of amphidromic points (red posts) half a wavelength apart.

This is the textbook reason the North Sea has three points (G.I. Taylor, 1921).

Turn reflection down (energy lost to friction at the far end): the points slide toward one coast and can end up on land, which is what a "degenerate" point is (S Norway, E Ireland).

What you are looking at

Gauge posts stand at the centre and 160 km north, east, south and west, each with a floating buoy.

The white arrow sits on the coast at the spot that has high water right now.

Turn on S2: the solar tide has its own point a little way off, so the centre buoy starts to bob slightly.

Colour = water height. Dark lines = cotidal lines: places that get high water at the same time, one line per lunar hour. They all meet at the amphidromic points, like spokes.

Watch the red crest run up one coast and back down the other, circling each point.

The yellow line is the side-view cut, running along the channel. The gauge traces are for the first point and the two coasts beside it.

Honest notes

The flat rotating sheet is exact close to any amphidromic point (first-order shape of a tide whose amplitude is zero there). Sizes are illustrative (1 m at the coast). Real seas wobble in an ellipse, not a circle. Horizontal currents are not drawn.

Two Kelvin waves are an exact solution of the linear rotating shallow-water equations in a straight channel. Wave speed c = sqrt(gH), rotation f = 2Ωsin(lat). The far closed end needs extra waves (Taylor's Poincaré modes), so it is not drawn; the channel just continues. The real North Sea is not a straight box, so the real points are not in a straight row.
